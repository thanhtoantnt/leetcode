Amortized analysis bounds the cost of a *sequence* of operations: one operation may be expensive, but expensive operations must be rare or pre-paid, so the average-per-operation is small — as a worst-case guarantee over sequences. No probability involved (that's the difference from Chapter 5's expected-time).

## 17.1 Aggregate analysis

Bound the total cost T(n) of any n operations; amortized cost = T(n)/n.

**Stack with MULTIPOP:** a single MULTIPOP can pop n items, so worst-case-per-op is O(n). But each item is popped at most once per push, so any n operations cost O(n) total → O(1) amortized each.

**Binary counter:** INCREMENT flips a run of trailing 1s — up to k bits in one call. But bit i flips only every 2^i increments: Σ ⌊n/2^i⌋ < 2n. Total O(n) → O(1) amortized. (Add DECREMENT and this collapses — the exercise worth doing: interleaving costs Θ(nk).)

## 17.2 The accounting method

Charge each operation a (possibly fictional) **amortized cost**. Cheap operations overpay; the surplus is banked as credit on the structure; expensive operations spend saved credit. Constraint: credit never goes negative — you can't pay for the future with money you don't have. Stack: charge PUSH 2 (1 for itself, 1 banked on the element), POP and MULTIPOP cost 0 — every pop is prepaid by its push.

## 17.3 The potential method

The bank formalized. Define a **potential function** Φ mapping the structure's state to a number (stack: number of elements; counter: number of 1-bits). Amortized cost of op i:

```
â_i = c_i + Φ(D_i) − Φ(D_{i−1})
```

Summing telescopes — Σâ = Σc + Φ(D_n) − Φ(D₀) — so as long as Φ never dips below its start, total amortized bounds total actual. Choosing Φ is an art (Chapter 14's subtree-aggregate instinct again): MULTIPOP has actual cost k′ and ΔΦ = −k′, so â = 0. The counter: â ≤ 2.

Same skeleton as physics energy bookkeeping: pay into potential when cheap, withdraw when expensive.

## 17.4 Dynamic tables

Why `list.append` is O(1) amortized in Python, and why hash tables resize. When the table fills, allocate double and copy — an O(n) spike. Aggregate/accounting/potential all prove inserts are O(1) amortized *with geometric growth*. Shrink symmetrically at ¼ full (not ½!) to avoid thrashing at the boundary — resize when load crosses a constant factor away from the trigger, never adjacent to it.

This is the quiet workhorse behind: dynamic arrays (`list`, `vector`), hash table resizing (Chapter 11's "keep α bounded"), and the two-stack queue (Exercise 17.3-6 — the transfer cost is prepaid by enqueues).
