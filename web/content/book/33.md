Computational geometry: cross products as the universal predicate, a sweep-line framework, and two classic divide-and-conquers (convex hull, closest pair).

## 33.1 The cross product is everything

The sign of the cross product (p₁→p₂) × (p₁→p₃) tells whether p₃ is left of, right of, or on the line — orientation without division, exact in integers. From it: do two segments straddle each other's lines (four cross products, plus boundary cases for collinear touching)? SEGMENTS-INTERSECT is entirely this. Every geometry problem starts by deciding which questions can be answered by signed areas.

## 33.2 The sweep line

Detect *whether any* pair of n segments intersects in O(n lg n) instead of n²: sweep x left to right, maintaining an ordered structure of segments the line currently crosses. Only segments **adjacent in the order** can newly intersect, so check just those at each event (endpoint or swap). General pattern — replace all pairs with neighbors-in-an-order-maintained-by-a-sweep — resurfaces in interval problems (Ch. 14's interval trees are the dynamic version) and in `intervals/`-style problems: sort by one coordinate, track a frontier structure.

## 33.3 Convex hull

Graham scan: sort by polar angle around the lowest point; walk, and whenever the turn is clockwise, pop the stack — the stack always holds a convex chain. O(n lg n), sort-dominated. The pop-while-wrong-turn loop is the monotonic-stack pattern from `stacks/` in geometric clothing.

## 33.4 Closest pair

Divide by a vertical line, recurse both halves (δ = best so far), then check pairs straddling the line — but only within the 2δ strip, scanned in y-order. The miracle: each point needs comparison with at most 7 following points in the strip (a δ×2δ box can't hold more at pairwise distance ≥ δ), so the combine step is linear and T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n) = Θ(n lg n). The punchline to remember: sometimes the cross-region candidates are provably few — bound the geometry, don't check all pairs.
