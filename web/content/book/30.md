The FFT: evaluate a degree-n polynomial at n points (and interpolate back) in Θ(n lg n), by choosing the *right* points — the complex roots of unity — so the same subproblems halve.

## The idea

A polynomial has two representations: coefficients and point-values (n+1 points determine it). Multiplying polynomials is Θ(n²) in coefficients but Θ(n) in point-values (pairwise multiply).

So: evaluate both at 2n points, multiply pointwise, interpolate. The whole trick is making evaluation/interpolation cheap — which works only at the roots of unity ω_n^k = e^{2πik/n}, because squaring them collapses: the even-indexed and odd-indexed powers of ω_n are each the n/2-th roots, twice over.

Split A(x) = A_even(x²) + x·A_odd(x²). Evaluating at all n roots of unity needs A_even and A_odd at the n/2 (squared) roots — half-size problems, recursively. T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n) = Θ(n lg n). Interpolation is the same algorithm with ω⁻¹ and a 1/n factor — the DFT matrix's inverse is its scaled conjugate.

## What it's for

Convolution, everywhere: big-integer multiplication (Chapter 30's capstone — breaking the Θ(n²) schoolbook method), string matching by correlation, signal processing, image blurring, computing binomial tails. The "multiply two sequences fast" primitive: `result = IFFT(FFT(a) · FFT(b))`. Any DP whose transition is a convolution over a range (e.g., some counting problems) drops a factor of n with this.

The transferable insight beyond the math: **choose your evaluation points so structure can be shared**. Divide-and-conquer with arbitrary splits re-solves subproblems; the roots of unity make the even/odd subproblem sets coincide, halving work genuinely. It's the same "make subproblems overlap on purpose" move as FFT-based string hashing.
