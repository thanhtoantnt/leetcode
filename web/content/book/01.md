Chapter 1 is not about any particular algorithm. It answers three questions: what an algorithm is, what kinds of problems they solve, and why the *choice* of algorithm matters as much as the hardware.

## 1.1 What an algorithm is

An algorithm is a well-defined procedure that turns input into output. More usefully: a **computational problem** states the desired input/output relationship; an **algorithm** is one specific procedure that achieves it.

The book's running example is sorting:

- **Input:** a sequence of *n* numbers
- **Output:** a permutation of that sequence in nondecreasing order
- **Instance:** one concrete input, e.g. ⟨31, 41, 59, 26, 41, 58⟩ → ⟨26, 31, 41, 41, 58, 59⟩

The numbers being sorted are the **keys**. Many programs call a sort as a subroutine, which is why the book spends so much of Part II on it.

**Correct** means: for *every* legal instance, the algorithm halts with the right answer. Incorrect algorithms (ones that can fail or loop) are usually discarded — except when the error rate is controllable. Chapter 31's primality tests are the later example of that exception.

You can specify an algorithm in English, in code, or in hardware. The only requirement is a precise procedure.

## What algorithms are for

Two properties keep showing up:

1. There are many candidate answers, almost all wrong. Finding a correct (or best) one is the work.
2. The problem actually comes up in practice.

Not every problem has an obvious set of candidates. The discrete Fourier transform produces coefficients, not a "choice among routes." The efficient algorithm for that is the FFT (Ch. 30).

The chapter also plants four later themes:

- **Data structures** — how you store data so access and updates are cheap. No one structure wins at everything.
- **Technique** — divide-and-conquer (Ch. 4), DP (Ch. 15), greedy (Ch. 16), amortized analysis (Ch. 17). Problems rarely announce which technique they want.
- **Hard problems** — NP-complete (Ch. 34). No efficient algorithm is known; if one exists for any of them, one exists for all of them. Traveling salesman is the poster child. If you can *prove* a problem is NP-complete, you stop hunting for an exact efficient algorithm and switch to an approximation (Ch. 35).
- **Parallelism** — clock speeds hit a heat wall, so chips grew cores (Ch. 27). For LeetCode this almost never matters; "faster algorithm" and "more cores" are different levers.

## 1.2 Algorithms as a technology

Suppose computers were infinitely fast and memory were free. You would still study algorithms — to prove a method *terminates* with the *correct* answer. In the real world, time and space are bounded, so you also care about how they grow with *n*.

The chapter's concrete punch:

- Insertion sort ≈ *c₁ n²*
- Merge sort ≈ *c₂ n lg n*

*c₁* is usually smaller than *c₂*, so insertion sort wins on tiny *n*. Once *n* is large, *lg n* vs *n* swamps the constants.

They make it unfair on purpose:

- Computer A: 10 billion instructions/sec, insertion sort in tight machine language (*2n²* instructions)
- Computer B: 10 million instructions/sec (1000× slower), merge sort compiled sloppily (*50 n lg n* instructions)
- Sort 10 million numbers

A takes ~5.6 hours. B takes < 20 minutes — about 17× faster, on hardware a thousand times slower. At 100 million numbers, insertion sort is > 23 days and merge sort is < 4 hours.

That is the thesis of the chapter: **an algorithm is a technology**, in the same bucket as CPUs, GUIs, and networks. A maps app still needs a shortest-path algorithm no matter how pretty the UI is. And as machines get bigger, *n* grows — which is exactly when the *n²* vs *n lg n* gap explodes.

## What the chapter wants you to do

The exercises are judgment, not coding:

- Name a real problem that needs sorting, or a convex hull
- Efficiency besides speed (memory, energy, disk seeks, network round-trips)
- How shortest-path and TSP are similar (a path through a graph) and different (visit every vertex vs source→target; TSP is NP-complete)
- When only the *best* answer will do vs when "pretty good" is enough

Problem 1-1 is a table: for each *f(n)* ∈ {lg *n*, √*n*, *n*, *n* lg *n*, *n²*, *n³*, *2ⁿ*, *n*!} and each time budget (second through century), what is the largest *n* you can handle at 1 µs per operation. Filling that table once is worth more than rereading the prose — you feel *2ⁿ* die at *n* ≈ 40 and *n*! die even earlier.
