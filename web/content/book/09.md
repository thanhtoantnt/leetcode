The order-statistics question: given n distinct numbers and i, return the i-th smallest — without sorting. Minimum, maximum, median are all special cases.

## 9.1 Min and max

Minimum takes n−1 comparisons, and that's optimal — every non-winner must lose a match (n−1 losers ⇒ n−1 comparisons). Finding min *and* max together, the pairs trick:

```text
[5, 1, 9, 3]  →  pairs (5,1) and (9,3)
pair-internal:  5>1,  9>3        (2 comparisons — halves the future work)
small vs min:   1 vs 3 → min 1   (1)
large vs max:   5 vs 9 → max 9   (1)
total 4 = ⌊3n/2⌋ − 2   vs naive 2(n−1) = 6
```

Each element enters either the min bracket or the max bracket — never both — and the pair-internal comparison decides which. That halving is the entire saving.

## 9.2 Selection in expected linear time — quickselect

Quicksort's partition, but recurse into **one side only**. On `[2,8,7,1,3,5,6,4]` (Chapter 7's partition, q lands with pivot 4 at slot 4):

```text
want the 4th smallest (i = 4)
partition:  [2 1 3 | 4 | 8 7 5 6]      k = 4 = q − p + 1 → the pivot IS the answer
                                    done in one Θ(n) pass, no recursion
want the 2nd smallest instead:
              [2 1 3 | 4 | …]  k=2 < 4 → recurse LEFT on [2,1,3] only
              partition that on 3: [2 1 | 3]  k=2 → recurse on [2,1] → answer 2
```

One recursive call per level instead of two — that asymmetry is the entire speedup over quicksort. The recurrence `E[T(n)] ≤ (2/n)·Σ_{k≥n/2} E[T(k)] + O(n)` closes by substitution with a big enough constant: **E[T(n)] = O(n)** — expected linear, for any order statistic, including the median. Worst case is still Θ(n²) (consistently terrible pivots), but randomization makes no input reliably bad. This is the go-to answer for "k-th largest" interviews — `queue/0215` in this repo, where the heap solution trades O(n) expected for O(n lg k) worst-case simple.

## 9.3 Selection in worst-case linear time — median-of-medians

The deterministic counterpart. Group the n elements into ⌈n/5⌉ columns of 5; insertion-sort each column; recursively find the median x of the column-medians; partition around x; recurse one side. The grid picture for 15 elements (each column sorted, medians in caps, x = median of medians):

```text
1   3   [7]        x = 7.  Every boxed median ≥ 7 (dark columns)
2   5   [8]        contributes 3 elements ≥ 7 (median + its two below)
4   6   [9]        ↘ the ⌈n/5⌉/2 good columns shade a huge region
5   9  [11]
[6] [9] [12]       ≥ 3n/10 − 6 elements ≥ x, mirrored below x
```

So x is a *guaranteed-good* pivot: at least `3n/10` elements land on each side, and the recursion keeps at most `7n/10 + 6`:

```
T(n) ≤ T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n)  →  T(n) = O(n)
```

n/5 + 7n/10 = 9n/10 < n — the two recursive calls' fractions sum to less than 1, so the levels' costs form a convergent geometric series (0.9^ℓ). Groups of 3 fail exactly because 1/3 + 2/3 = 1: the recursion no longer shrinks, the series stops converging. Constants are terrible; nobody implements this outside proofs. Its moral: a pivot that's *provably* decent costs less than a pivot that's *actually good*.

The chapter's shape — randomize for expected speed, or engineer for worst-case proof — repeats across the book (hashing vs perfect hashing, universal hashes).
