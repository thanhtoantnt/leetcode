Matrix algorithms: solving Ax = b, inverting matrices, and the least-squares story. For interviews this chapter is mostly background; the ideas that transfer are the decompositions.

## 28.1 LUP decomposition

Factor PA = LU (permuted lower × upper triangular) once, then solve triangular systems by back-substitution — each solve is O(n²) against the factorization's Θ(n³). The pivot permutation P is what buys numerical stability (partial pivoting: never divide by a tiny number). Same economy as computing an MST index once and querying many times: pay the cube once, solve in squares.

Gaussian elimination viewed through this lens = computing L and U in place, row by row.

## 28.2 Inversion

A⁻¹ exists iff A is nonsingular, and inversion is matrix multiplication in disguise (blockwise inversion reduces to multiplications) — so inversion has Strassen's Θ(n^2.81) too. But you almost never want the inverse: x = A⁻¹b is slower and less stable than LUP-solving. Inverting to solve is the classic "technically correct, practically wrong" move.

## 28.3 Symmetric positive-definite and least squares

For SPD matrices, **Cholesky** (A = LLᵀ, no pivoting needed) halves the work and is the engine under the hood of many numerical libraries. The least-squares punchline: an overdetermined system Ax = b (more equations than unknowns) has the normal equations AᵀAx = Aᵀb, whose matrix AᵀA is SPD — solve that, get the best-fit x. Every linear regression is this chapter running.
